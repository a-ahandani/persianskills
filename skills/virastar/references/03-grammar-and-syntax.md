# Persian Grammar and Syntax

Contents: Editorial goal; Subject–verb agreement; Pronouns, clitics, and reference; Verb valency and prepositions; Word order and information structure; Ezafe chains and overloaded noun phrases; Relative and complement clauses with `که`; Coordination and parallelism; Tense, aspect, and time reference; Active, passive, and first person; Nominalization and light-verb constructions; Sentence boundaries; Ambiguity tests; Grammar review output.

## Editorial goal

Correct grammar is necessary but not sufficient. A grammatical sentence may still be ambiguous, overloaded, badly ordered, or misleading in an academic argument. Review grammar at three levels:

1. **well-formedness** — morphology, agreement, valency, coordination;
2. **recoverability** — whether pronouns, ellipses, and omitted subjects have one clear interpretation;
3. **information structure** — whether the sentence foregrounds the intended topic, contrast, condition, or conclusion.

Do not mechanically force Persian into English word order or English agreement patterns.

## Subject–verb agreement

### Human and animate plural subjects

A human plural subject normally takes a plural verb in formal academic Persian:

- preferred: `پژوهشگران این فرضیه را آزمودند.`
- suspect: `پژوهشگران این فرضیه را آزمود.`

Check coordinated subjects as well. A singular verb may be acceptable only when the coordination denotes one conceptual unit or the grammatical head is genuinely singular.

### Nonhuman plural subjects

Standard Persian permits variation with nonhuman plurals. A singular verb can present the plural as a class, mass, or non-individuated set; a plural verb can individuate or foreground the members. Therefore, do not import the English rule that every morphologically plural subject must take a plural verb.

Examples whose acceptability depends on intended construal:

- `داده‌ها در جدول ۳ گزارش شده است.`
- `داده‌ها در سه آزمایش مستقل گردآوری شده‌اند.`

Review for semantic fit and manuscript consistency rather than marking one form universally wrong. Human referents, personalized entities, and explicitly counted items normally favor plural agreement.

### Hidden subject changes

Flag a sentence when two coordinated clauses appear to share one subject but the second verb actually requires another:

- risky: `مدل آموزش داده شد و دقت را محاسبه کرد.`

Determine whether the model, researcher, or evaluation procedure computes the metric; repair agency explicitly.

## Pronouns, clitics, and reference

Persian permits omitted subjects and attached pronouns, which can create ambiguity in technical prose.

Check every form such as:

- `آن` / `آن‌ها`;
- `این` / `این‌ها`;
- `وی` / `او`;
- possessive/object clitics such as `ـش` and `ـشان`;
- omitted subjects across sentence boundaries;
- `مورد اخیر`, `روش مذکور`, `این مسئله`, `این امر`.

Ask: can a reader identify exactly one antecedent without rereading? If not, repeat the technical noun. Repetition is preferable to referential ambiguity in methods, definitions, and claims.

Do not replace every repeated noun with a pronoun merely for stylistic variety.

## Verb valency and prepositions

Verify that the verb or predicate licenses its complement and preposition. Translation from English often produces non-native pairings.

Review candidates such as:

- `منجر شدن به`;
- `تأثیر گذاشتن بر`;
- `وابسته بودن به`;
- `مقایسه کردنِ الف با ب`;
- `تمایز گذاشتن میان/بین الف و ب`;
- `استناد کردن به`;
- `مبتنی بودن بر`;
- `سازگار بودن با`.

Do not “correct” collocations from intuition alone when the discipline uses a stable conventional form. Check nearby usage, the terminology register, and authoritative dictionaries when necessary.

## Word order and information structure

Persian has a canonical subject–object–verb tendency but flexible constituent order. Preserve marked order when it expresses contrast or focus. Edit when long interruptions make the subject–predicate relation difficult to recover.

Common problems:

- a very long subject followed by a distant verb;
- stacked parentheticals before the main predicate;
- a condition or exception attached to the wrong proposition;
- a modifier placed where it can govern two different nouns;
- `تنها`, `فقط`, `نیز`, `حتی`, or negation with unclear scope.

Scope-sensitive example:

- `این روش تنها در داده‌های آموزشی دقت را افزایش داد.`

Possible readings include “only this method,” “only on training data,” or “only accuracy.” Move `تنها` next to the constituent it limits.

## Ezafe chains and overloaded noun phrases

Long ezafe chains are grammatical but hard to parse. Flag chains that contain several abstract nouns, embedded English terms, or more than one plausible attachment.

Instead of blindly splitting every chain, identify the semantic relations and choose among:

- a finite clause;
- an appositive;
- two shorter noun phrases;
- a colon or list;
- a defined technical term.

Example:

- dense: `روش ارزیابی عملکرد مدل پیش‌بینی رفتار یادگیرنده`
- clearer alternatives depend on meaning: `روش ارزیابیِ عملکردِ مدلِ پیش‌بینی رفتار یادگیرنده` may still be ambiguous; use a clause such as `روشی برای ارزیابی عملکرد مدلی که رفتار یادگیرنده را پیش‌بینی می‌کند` when that is the intended structure.

Do not add visible ezafe marks throughout ordinary prose merely to solve a syntactic problem.

## Relative and complement clauses with `که`

`که` is highly productive. The problem is not the word itself but unclear nesting.

Flag:

- several nested `که` clauses;
- a relative clause whose antecedent is unclear;
- a complement clause mistaken for a relative clause;
- a delayed verb that forces rereading;
- a clause that contains two unrelated claims.

Repair by separating propositions or naming the antecedent. Do not impose a numeric ban on occurrences of `که`.

## Coordination and parallelism

Items joined by `و`, `یا`, `اما`, `بلکه`, `نه‌تنها ... بلکه ...`, or a colon/list should have compatible grammatical and logical form.

Check:

- noun + clause coordination;
- infinitive + finite verb coordination;
- inconsistent voice or tense in a procedural list;
- a shared preposition that does not fit every item;
- `یا` where alternatives are not mutually exclusive;
- `و` where a causal or contrastive relation is intended.

Parallelism is especially important in research questions, hypotheses, contribution lists, objectives, and table headings.

## Tense, aspect, and time reference

Do not enforce one tense for an entire section. Choose tense by rhetorical function:

- established knowledge or what a paper states: often present;
- completed actions in the current study: often past or present perfect, according to house style;
- procedures described as a reproducible protocol: present or past can both be defensible;
- figure/table content: commonly present;
- future work: future or modal construction.

Flag unmotivated switching that changes chronology or claim status. Preserve aspect distinctions such as completed action, ongoing state, habitual process, and prior event.

## Active, passive, and first person

Do not categorically ban any of these.

Prefer the construction that makes agency and responsibility clear:

- active/first person can clarify a deliberate methodological decision;
- passive can foreground the procedure or object when the actor is irrelevant;
- agentless passive is risky when responsibility for selection, exclusion, coding, or interpretation matters.

Review repetitive passives and `توسط` chains as clarity problems, not automatic grammatical errors.

## Nominalization and light-verb constructions

Persian legitimately uses compound predicates and light verbs. Do not replace every `انجام دادن`, `ارائه کردن`, or `مورد بررسی قرار دادن` with a simple verb by rule.

Flag a construction when it:

- obscures the actor;
- adds empty words;
- creates several nested abstract nouns;
- weakens a precise technical relation;
- repeats the same light verb monotonously.

Possible concise repairs include `بررسی کردن`, `سنجیدن`, `آزمودن`, `گزارش کردن`, or `محاسبه کردن`, but only when the denotation remains the same.

## Sentence boundaries

Flag:

- comma splices between independent propositions;
- fragments with no recoverable predicate;
- sentences containing multiple claims that need separate evidence;
- semicolons used only as decoration;
- bullet items that do not complete the lead-in grammatically.

A long sentence is not wrong merely because it is long. Split it when the logical relations or referents cannot be tracked reliably.

## Ambiguity tests

For a suspected sentence, test explicitly:

1. What is the grammatical subject of each verb?
2. What does each pronoun or demonstrative refer to?
3. What is the scope of negation, quantifiers, hedges, and focus particles?
4. Which noun does each modifier or relative clause modify?
5. Are condition, cause, contrast, and consequence signaled correctly?
6. Does punctuation support the same parse as the words?
7. Could a technically competent reader derive a materially different interpretation?

If two readings remain, label the edit `MEANING-SENSITIVE` and present alternatives rather than guessing.

## Grammar review output

For each grammar/syntax finding, distinguish:

- **ungrammatical form**;
- **grammatical but ambiguous form**;
- **grammatical but unnecessarily difficult form**;
- **house-style inconsistency**.

Only the first is unconditionally an error. The other categories require evidence and calibrated severity.
